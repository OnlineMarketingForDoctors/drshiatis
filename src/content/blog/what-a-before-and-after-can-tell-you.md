A before and after gallery is the first thing most people look at and the last thing most people
know how to read. It is worth explaining how these photographs are made, because once you know
that, you can tell a great deal from a gallery, including when one is being used to mislead you.

## How a clinical photograph is supposed to be taken

Standardised, which is a dull word doing a lot of work. The same camera, the same distance, the
same lens, the same lighting, the same background, the same posture, the same set of angles, and
underwear of a similar cut. Before and after should differ in exactly one respect: the surgery.

That is the standard. It is also why clinical photographs look flat and unflattering compared with
anything else on a surgeon's website. They are not meant to be flattering. They are a measurement.

## What to look for

- **The same angles, both sides.** A gallery showing a front view before and a three-quarter view
  after is comparing two different things, and the three-quarter view is usually the kinder one.
- **Consistent lighting.** Hard light from above flatters an abdomen enormously. If the after
  photograph is lit differently from the before, part of the result is the lighting.
- **The same posture.** Shoulders back, hips square, arms in the same place. A slight turn or an
  arched back changes a waist more than many operations do.
- **Faces and whole frames.** Tight crops can hide as much as they show. A gallery of nothing but
  close-ups is worth asking about.
- **A range of starting points.** If every patient in a gallery was slim and young to begin with,
  the gallery tells you about patient selection rather than about surgery.

## What a photograph genuinely cannot tell you

**How long after.** Swelling takes months to settle and scars take a year or more to fade. A result
photographed at six weeks looks different from the same result at twelve months, in both
directions: the shape is often better early and the scars are always worse. Any honest gallery
tells you when each photograph was taken.

**How that person started.** Skin quality, previous pregnancies, weight history, how much tissue
there was to work with. Two people having the same operation from different starting points get
different results, and neither is the surgeon's doing.

**Whether it was comfortable.** Nothing in a photograph tells you about the recovery, the pain, the
weeks off work, or how the person felt about it at three months.

**Whether it is yours.** This is the important one. A photograph of somebody else's result is
evidence that a surgeon can produce that result in that anatomy. It is not a prediction about
yours.

## Our own gallery

The photographs on this site are of Mr Shiatis's own patients, published with written consent,
cropped but otherwise untouched. No retouching, no stock images, no borrowed cases.

Where a set was photographed on the day of surgery rather than after healing, it says so on the
page, because an immediate post-operative photograph and a settled result are not the same claim
and should not be presented as though they were.

Bring the ones that look like you to a consultation. The useful conversation is not "can you do
this" but "what can my own anatomy actually get to", and that is a conversation that needs you in
the room.
